This is a Python implementation of the unbalanced  mountain building model proposed by Brendan Meade (2010).

In an ideal sense, the long term uplift of a thrust system should reflect the combined effects of interseismic and coseismic deformation, which I will refer to as a balanced system. In a simple thrust fault geometry, the cumulative uplift forms a broad block that translates into a concave bedrock river profile predicted by the stream power equation. But imagine if the coseismic events, for some reason, never catch up to the timescale over which a river adjusts its channel slope. 

Consider a bedrock river traversing a thrust system characterized by an absence of coseismic slip. At the transition depth between the locked and creeping regimes, interseismic deformation drives localized uplift along the x-axis. Consequently, the river profile will record the signature of this deformation.

Consequently, bedrock river profiles offer insights into the partitioning between coseismic and interseismic activity. This is pertinent to the Himalayas, a region characterized by infrequent major seismic events and a hypothesized significant accumulation of slip deficit. Meade (2010) used a planar fault model extending continuously from the free surface to depth. Under the assumption of a uniform long-term slip rate, this geometry yields a characteristic uplift signature.

However, the Himalayan fault geometry is complicated by ramp structures which are likely to produce localized uplift regardless of whether the earthquake cycle is balanced. As slip drives the hanging wall over these deep geologic bends, the crust is forced upward. To simulate the localized uplift generated by these ramp structures, one can treat the fold itself as a source of displacement. Souter and Hager (1997) demonstrate that we can model the ramp system by introducing discrete surfaces along the fold's axial planes and assigning them dislocation values, much like the fault. This approach works because the deformation gradient tensor, which mathematically describes how material changes shape, is identical for a kink band and a fault. Effectively, as the hanging wall translates across the bend, the rock undergoes a rigid body rotation through the hinge. We can model this sharp change in trajectory as slip on the axial plane.

Let’s use this concept to build a minimal representation of the Himalayan fault geometry.

Grandin et al. (2012) argue that the interseismic uplift peak lies exactly where long-term uplift is highest based on InSAR observation, even though a simple flat–ramp–flat dislocation model predicts that these peaks should be offset by ~20 km. They interpret this mismatch as evidence that the mid-crustal ramp must have recently migrated southward. But once ramp-fold kinematics are included, uplift becomes more distributed across the entire ramp, which reduces the predicted spatial offset. This makes the observed coincidence between interseismic and long-term peaks less diagnostic of ramp migration than in the simple dislocation-only model.
